# C10 — GPL-3.0 and the App Store: a decision, not a task

**Owner: the owner.** It is a licensing decision about their own work.

**RULED 2026-08-27 by the owner: option 3, dual licence.** GPL-3.0-or-later for the
repository; a separate grant for the binaries. Research below was done after the ruling and
did not contradict it — but it changes what the *risk* actually is, and produces one action
that has to happen before the first outside pull request, not before the first submission.

## The conflict, stated plainly

`SPEC.md` line 4: **GPL-3.0-or-later (copyleft, open source). Public GitHub repo.**

The Free Software Foundation's position is that the App Store's terms are **incompatible with
GPLv3**. The App Store imposes usage rules — device limits, DRM — on everyone who downloads an
app, and GPLv3 forbids adding restrictions beyond the licence. Apps have been pulled over this
before; VLC is the well-known case.

**This is not a technicality that goes away by ignoring it.** It is a conflict between two
things the project has already committed to.

## What tomorrow has to produce

A ruling, and it belongs in `SPEC.md` rather than in a plan. The options, so far as they are
known before research:

1. **Keep GPL-3.0, do not ship to the App Store.** TestFlight for a private group, or source
   distribution and let people build it. Costs reach; costs nothing else. The spec already
   says the hard stop abandons unmerged work cleanly, and "not on the App Store" is a
   defensible end state for a personal tool.
2. **Relicense.** The owner holds the copyright — the agent's contributions are work made for
   them — so this is theirs to do. A permissive licence (MIT, Apache-2.0) removes the conflict
   entirely. It also gives up the copyleft the spec chose on purpose, and that reason should
   be re-read before it is discarded.
3. **Dual licence.** GPL-3.0 for the repository, a separate grant for the App Store binary.
   Legitimate and common, and it needs the owner to be comfortable being the licensor of both.
4. **GPL-2.0-or-later or LGPL.** Sometimes claimed to be less restrictive here. **Verify
   rather than assume** — the anti-tivoisation clause is a GPLv3 addition, but the FSF's
   objection to the App Store's usage rules is not solely about that clause.

## What the agent should do first, tomorrow

**Research before recommending.** The last two times a decision was put to the owner without
investigating first, the investigation collapsed the question — the Todoist project attribution
needed no schema change, and the time-zone edge case turned out to be one other systems
disagree about. This one has the same shape: a well-documented question with real precedent, on
which an unresearched recommendation would be worth very little.

Sources worth reading rather than recalling: the FSF's own statements on the App Store, the
VLC removal and what actually caused it, and how currently-shipping GPL-licensed iOS apps
handle it — if any do.

# Research, 2026-08-27

Done at the owner's instruction and after the ruling. The brief said *"research before
recommending"* and named three things to read: the FSF's position, what actually caused the
VLC removal, and how shipping GPL apps handle it.

## The finding that matters: nobody audits, somebody complains

**Apple did not detect a licence conflict and act on it.** In both known cases the removal
followed a **copyright complaint from a rights holder**:

- **VLC**, pulled January 2011, after developer **Rémi Denis-Courmont — who had written much
  of the code — petitioned Apple to remove it**, on the grounds that App Store distribution
  breached the GPL under which *he* had contributed. Apple had the notification for over two
  months before acting.
- **GNU Go**, pulled earlier, after a letter from the **FSF**.

Denis-Courmont himself said of Apple's motive *"we will probably never know the truth."*

**This reframes the whole question.** The exposure was never "Apple will notice." It was
"someone who holds copyright in this code can object, and Apple will comply." A licence
conflict with no complainant is a conflict nobody enforces.

## Which is exactly why dual licensing works

**The GPL binds licensees, not the author.** A copyright holder is not licensing the work to
themselves and cannot breach their own licence. Distributing the same code under GPL-3.0 in
the repository and under a different grant in a binary is not a loophole — it is the ordinary
consequence of owning the copyright, and it is long-established practice.

So the ruling is sound, and its soundness rests entirely on **one condition**.

## The condition, and the one action this research produces

> **Dual licensing survives only as long as the owner holds copyright in all of it.**

The moment an outside contribution is merged under GPL-3.0 and nobody has granted broader
rights, that contributor's copyright is in the tree — and the binary can no longer be licensed
under anything else without their permission.

**That is precisely how VLC's situation arose**: not through carelessness about licences, but
through a contributor who held rights and later objected.

Two things follow, and neither is about the App Store:

**1. State the copyright position now.** `CLAUDE.md` records that the agent authors everything
as work made for the owner. That belongs in the repository as well as in a working file, so
the ownership of the existing tree is not something anyone has to reconstruct later.

**2. Decide what happens at the first outside pull request — before it arrives.** The public
repo invites them. The options are a **CLA** (contributors grant the owner rights broad enough
to relicense) or **copyright assignment** (the FSF's own approach for GNU projects, and the
strongest position). Without one, accepting a single PR quietly ends the dual licence.

**This is not urgent because of TestFlight. It is urgent because a stranger can open a pull
request tomorrow**, and the cost of declining one for licensing reasons after the fact is
much higher than a `CONTRIBUTING.md` that says the terms up front.

## Two smaller things the research settles

**No GPL-licensed third-party code or assets may enter the binary.** `CLAUDE.md` already
forbids third-party dependencies without a delta, which covers code by accident. **`D24`'s
alert sounds are the first real test of it**: CC0 and CC-BY are both fine in a
differently-licensed binary, and the attribution the owner has already ruled on satisfies
CC-BY's only condition. A GPL-licensed sound would not be fine, and would be an easy thing to
pick up without noticing.

**The binary grant owes no source offer.** GPL-3.0 §6's obligation to provide source attaches
to conveying *under the GPL*. The App Store binary is not conveyed under it, so the obligation
does not follow it there — and the source is public anyway, which makes the point academic
but worth stating so nobody re-derives it.

## What this does not cover

**Which licence the binaries carry.** "Something else" is now a decision with three sane
answers — an ordinary proprietary EULA, a permissive licence like Apache-2.0, or simply
Apple's standard EULA — and choosing between them is about what the owner wants to permit,
not about the GPL conflict, which is already resolved by holding the copyright.

**None of this is legal advice**, and the two removals cited are the only well-documented
precedents; both are fifteen years old and Apple's terms have changed since.
